# [Florida’s drowned landscapes offer new clues to ancient coastal life](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/environment/article/mapping-texas-florida-coasts-ancient-american-archaeology)
*Updated: 2026-10-23T00:00:00+00:00*

Archaeologists are mapping submerged landscapes between Texas and Florida to locate evidence of Indigenous communities whose homelands disappeared beneath rising seas. Recent reporting describes a stone tool recovered from buried soil offshore Florida, while established research shows why these underwater settings can preserve an unusually valuable record of human life.

![Image accompanying the original article](https://i.natgeofe.com/n/34f9e901-cf42-4f79-b814-3ee87919b76f/GettyImages-1157365028copy_16x9.jpg?w=1200)

## A discovery beneath the seabed

National Geographic reports that archaeologist Morgan Smith recovered a stone scraper from an ancient land surface five miles offshore in December 2024. The account describes the tool as about 7,000 years old and reports an older piece of wood beneath it. The discovery is a window onto a drowned landscape, rather than new evidence by itself of the earliest arrival of people in the Americas. [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/environment/article/mapping-texas-florida-coasts-ancient-american-archaeology).

The same reporting describes researchers following buried river channels and surveying offshore Texas, using acoustic data to identify places where ancient soils might survive. Sediment cores can then test those interpretations; a promising landform is not yet a confirmed settlement. [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/environment/article/mapping-texas-florida-coasts-ancient-american-archaeology).

## Mapping places that development could disturb

The wider effort has an established research and preservation purpose. Florida State University announced in May 2024 that a team had received roughly $1.5 million for a five-year collaboration with the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management. The project combines existing offshore survey records with new sonar surveys and sediment sampling to model where submerged archaeological sites may remain intact. [Florida State University](https://news.fsu.edu/news/arts-humanities/2024/05/01/fsu-researcher-receives-1-5m-in-funding-to-partner-with-the-bureau-of-ocean-energy-management-to-identify-submerged-landscapes/).

FSU said the findings would inform survey guidelines and measures to avoid damage from offshore development. Tribal communities are involved in planning, implementation and interpretation, including decisions about which ancestral landscapes deserve examination and protection. [Florida State University](https://news.fsu.edu/news/arts-humanities/2024/05/01/fsu-researcher-receives-1-5m-in-funding-to-partner-with-the-bureau-of-ocean-energy-management-to-identify-submerged-landscapes/).

## What submerged sites already establish

At Page-Ladson, in Florida’s Aucilla River, researchers documented stone tools and mastodon remains in undisturbed deposits. Their 2016 study used 71 radiocarbon ages to place human activity around 14,550 calendar years ago. The evidence indicates that people butchered or scavenged a mastodon beside a pond, establishing an early human presence in the region without resolving every question about migration routes. [Halligan and colleagues, Science Advances](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/pre-clovis-occupation-14550-years-ago-at-the-page-ladson-site-flo/).

A different example comes from Manasota Key Offshore. Florida’s archaeological authorities report a prehistoric burial place preserved within the remains of a freshwater pond now under the sea. Wooden stakes associated with burial practices dated to more than 7,200 years ago. The site demonstrates that delicate archaeological deposits can survive inundation and help researchers reconstruct environments that no longer exist above water. [Florida Department of State](https://dos.fl.gov/historical/archaeology/projects/manasota-key-offshore/).

Together, these records explain the stakes of the new surveys: identifying preserved deposits could reveal how coastal communities lived and adapted as shorelines moved. Whether any particular offshore target contains such evidence remains a question for investigation.
